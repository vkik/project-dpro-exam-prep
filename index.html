    Projects in the development sector

    Development and humanitarian organisations change the world through projects. Their staff write proposals, plan, implement, monitor and evaluate, and communities invest their own time and trust in the work. Yet project management is rarely treated as a strategic priority. Organisations hire technical specialists in health, education or WASH and then ask them to manage projects, often with little project management training. The Guide exists to close that gap.

    The Guide follows one case study throughout: the Delta River. River water quality has fallen because of fecal waste, household and business waste, and poorly treated wastewater. Crops, incomes and health are suffering, especially among poor families and children under five. The project you will meet again and again is a latrine building project for this community.

    What makes development projects different

    All projects share challenges of time, budget, quality, scope and risk. Development projects add their own. They deliver tangible outputs, but also less tangible social and behavioural change. They tackle complex problems of poverty, inequality and injustice. They work in hard contexts with limited resources, insecurity and unstable politics, through complex webs of partners, ministries and community groups. In this sector the approach is as important as the outcome, so participation and rights-based approaches matter. Transferring knowledge to the target population is a priority in every phase, and funding environments keep changing.

    Why project management matters

    The Guide gives three reasons. Projects are the agents of change: if a project fails to deliver its outputs, the changes hoped for at outcome, program and portfolio level cannot happen. Projects sit in a wider context of programs and portfolios. And there is a growing demand for accountability, which flows in three directions.

    Upward accountability is to donors and taxpayers, and to the organisation's own program and portfolio levels. Downward accountability is to the communities served; it includes the Do No Harm principle, which asks how an intervention might hurt the people it targets, especially in conflict settings. Horizontal accountability is to peers: the other NGOs, CBOs and agencies working in the same area, and the coordination platforms that connect them.

    Projects, the triple constraint, programs and portfolios

    A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. Project management is planning, organising and managing resources to achieve specific goals, outcomes and outputs. The project manager is responsible for the project's success, but not for doing all its work personally. Often they must lead people over whom they have no formal authority.

    Every project lives inside the triple constraint: scope/quality, cost/resources and time/schedule. The three sides are linked. If a donor asks you to serve 12,000 families instead of 10,000, the scope grows, so cost and time must grow too. The project manager keeps the triangle in balance.

    Each constraint can be classed as inflexible or adaptable (may concede). A winterization project that must deliver blankets before winter has an inflexible time constraint. A donor who forbids budget changes makes the budget inflexible. When the budget is fixed and exchange rates rise, the scope concedes, and fewer kits are bought. Agree these priorities with stakeholders early, because renegotiating after launch is very hard.

    A program is a group of related projects managed in a coordinated way, so that the whole delivers more than the parts would alone. Projects align under a program by geographic area, sector, objectives, funding, target populations and management. A portfolio is the full set of programs and projects, managed by a Country Director or senior executives. Portfolio management balances limited resources against strategy and decides whether to stop or redirect work. In short: projects deliver outputs, programs deliver outcomes and benefits, and portfolios ensure strategic fit.

    Projects come from four main sources: an INGO working through implementing partners, a stand-alone project paid from unrestricted funds, a grant, or a project designed under a program.

    The project manager's competencies

    Good project management is both art and science. The art is about people: leading, motivating, communicating and resolving conflict. The science is technical: planning, estimating, measuring and controlling. The Guide tells the story of David, a skilled planner whose team kept quitting because he only spoke to them about deadlines and mistakes. Strong science could not save him from weak art.

    The Project DPro competency model has four areas: PM Technical (the science), Leadership/Interpersonal (the art), Personal/Self-Management (organisation, time management, analytical thinking), and Development Sector Specific (understanding the sector, its stakeholders, partners and pressures, and cultural sensitivity). Annex I lists 31 competencies in total. The level a manager needs rises with the project's size, complexity and risk. The Guide itself concentrates on the PM Technical and Development Sector Specific areas.

    The phase model and decision gates

    Many organisations draw a project lifecycle: a circle, a line or a spiral showing how a project moves from start to end. Project DPro offers its own phase model, which can sit inside an organisation's lifecycle or stand alone. It has five phases.

    In Identification and Definition, the team defines needs, explores opportunities, analyses the environment and designs options. The decisions made here set the strategic and operational framework for everything that follows. In Project Set Up, the project is officially authorized; its main parameters are defined and communicated, and a governance structure is put in place. In Project Planning, the team turns earlier documents into a detailed implementation plan with supporting plans for MEAL, supply chain and more. In Project Implementation, the manager leads the day-to-day work: the team, the issues, and keeping all parts of the plan working together. In Project Closure, deliverables are confirmed with beneficiaries, lessons are collected, and administrative, financial and contractual closure is completed.

    The phases are not a strict relay race. They overlap and interact: you do not finish one, stop, and start the next. Closure, for example, happens at the end, but closure thinking begins in Identification and Definition. The model is also deliberately balanced. Organisations often pour effort into design and implementation and neglect the rest; the Guide asks for similar care in every phase.

    MEAL is everywhere

    The first edition had a phase called Monitoring, Evaluation and Control (MEC). The second edition widened it to Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability and Learning (MEAL) and removed it as a separate phase. MEAL is now a cross-cutting theme that appears in every phase. Remember this change: the exam likes it.

    Decision gates

    A decision gate is a checkpoint where the team asks one question: are we still doing the right project, and are we still doing it in the right way? At each gate there are three possible outcomes: continue as planned, change the project, or stop it. Stopping is not necessarily a failure; if the need has gone or the project is no longer feasible, closing it is the right decision.

    In the phase model, gates are drawn as triangles between the phases, but the number is only illustrative. Longer, more complex or riskier projects need more gates. Gates are tied to the governance structure and to the project manager's tolerances: when everyone knows who can decide what, gates run smoothly.

    Why bother? Gates protect the organisation from the "perfected but rejected" scenario, where thousands of dollars are spent perfecting a proposal that key stakeholders never support. They give a framework for control, and they bring in multiple perspectives and build shared ownership. Regular gates, for example at the start of each implementation year, keep the project focused on the need it was created for and check that the original context and assumptions still hold.

    The Delta River project shows three early gates: the Concept Note, the Project Proposal and Government Authorization. A concept note or proposal can be approved, rejected, or approved with changes. Gates inside a phase must be closed before the next phase begins. If government approval is still pending and the team moves into Set Up anyway, it risks spending money on a project that was never authorised.

    Sometimes the context changes suddenly, as when conflict puts the team in danger. Then an Emergency Decision Gate is used to decide quickly whether to continue or close the project. That decision is usually above the project manager's tolerance, so it is made with the governance structure.

    How each phase chapter is organised

    The Guide describes every phase in the same way: an introduction, the key outputs, what it means in practice, the inputs, and the processes and tools. The next five chapters of this reader follow that pattern, so watch for the outputs and inputs of each phase.

    Identification and Definition

    Every project starts as an idea, a need or an opportunity. This phase answers the critical question: are we doing the right project? Get it wrong here and the project stays wrong, however well it is planned and run. It is also the cheapest time to change anything: before staff are hired and money is spent, changing objectives, schedule or budget costs little.

    The phase has four key outputs: a stakeholder analysis, the project logical frame, a project proposal to obtain funding, and a high-level project charter. Its inputs may include the project concept, a Terms of Reference from a request for proposal, lessons learned from similar projects, and the program Theory of Change. Common decision gates here are the needs assessment, the concept note and the proposal. A concept note is a short, high-level overview written to get feedback before committing resources to a full proposal.

    The Guide wants this phase to be participatory. It takes more time, but it lets stakeholders take control of their own development, strengthens the design, and builds ownership. The project manager may not even be hired yet, so the program manager, the MEAL team and the support teams (supply chain, HR, finance) carry the knowledge forward.

    Defining needs and collecting data

    Needs assessment begins with data. Before collecting anything, plan the purpose, the resources, the people needed and the timeframe: treat data collection as a small project of its own. Always ask, "How will this data be used?" If there is no good answer, don't collect it. Reports that sit on a shelf waste money and raise false expectations.

    There are three types of data. Secondary data (reports, statistics, other NGOs' assessments) is cheap and fast and should be used first, though it may be outdated or unreliable. Primary quantitative data (household surveys, standardised tests, measurements) can be counted, scaled up and generalised, but it misses depth. Primary qualitative data (focus groups, key informant interviews, ranking exercises, mapping) gives depth and context, but it cannot be generalised and needs skilled staff. A mixed methods approach combines them.

    Because needs are subjective, the Guide recommends triangulation: checking findings from more than two sources or methods. If two of three agree, confidence grows; if all three conflict, rethink the question. Avoid telling communities what your organisation usually funds before you assess them, or they may describe their needs to fit your programs.

    Jonathan Bradshaw's four types of need help test whether a need is "real". A felt need is what people say they want. An expressed need shows in what they do, such as long waiting lists or families digging garbage pits. A normative need is measured against expert standards, such as pollution above a Ministry of Health limit. A comparative need comes from comparing with others, such as a nearby town with more fish because it has sanitation. When all four point the same way, the need is real.

    Analysing needs: where we are and where we want to be

    Needs analysis has two parts. Current state analysis asks where we are now: the problem, its causes, and the stakeholders. Future state analysis asks where we want to be: the change we expect, the risks, and whether it can be sustained.

    Stakeholders can make or break a project. The Guide's stakeholder management system has five components: identification and analysis (this phase), engagement (Set Up), communications (Planning), and revision (continuously). It groups stakeholders into six categories. Users benefit directly. Governance stakeholders care how the project is run: boards, sponsors, auditors, regulators and funders. Providers do the work: staff, partners, contractors, suppliers. Influencers can change the project's direction: media, officials, community leaders. Dependents need something from the project other than its main product, often another project. Sustainers keep the benefits going afterwards, like the Delta River municipality maintaining the latrines. A group can sit in more than one category, and categories change over time.

    Two tools analyse stakeholders. A Venn diagram is drawn from one stakeholder's point of view: the size of each circle shows power or influence, and the distance between circles shows how close the working relationship is. A Stakeholder Analysis Matrix adds written detail on interests, influence, relationships and actions; it is a living document, reviewed at decision gates.

    To understand the problem, build a problem tree. Put the core problem in the middle, its causes below and its effects above, asking "what causes that?" at each step. Then turn it into an objectives tree, where every negative statement becomes a positive one; the cause-and-effect links become means-to-end links. An alternatives tree then shows which parts the project will tackle and which are out of scope. Scope decisions weigh nine criteria: needs priority, what others are doing, appropriateness, institutional capacity, resources, cost-effectiveness, technical feasibility and sustainability, internal priorities, and portfolio fit. When the donor, the data and the community disagree, the project manager does not decide alone: governance and stakeholders must be involved.

    The logical framework

    The logframe shows how the intervention leads to change. Project DPro uses four levels. Activities use inputs to produce deliverables. Outputs are the deliverables themselves, such as latrines built and people trained. Outcomes are the change at beneficiary level, such as improved access to and use of latrines. The goal is the highest-level impact, such as better river water quality, to which the project only contributes.

    The vertical logic reads as if-then: if inputs are provided, activities happen; if activities happen, outputs are produced; if outputs are produced, outcomes occur; and outcomes contribute to the goal. The project manager is primarily responsible for activities and outputs, because those are within the project's control.

    The horizontal logic adds three columns: indicators, means of verification and assumptions. Assumptions are conditions, often outside the project's control, that must hold true for the logic to work, read as "if AND then": if the outputs are delivered and the assumptions hold, then the outcomes follow. They matter most at output and outcome level, and they are a good starting point for risk analysis. An indicator measures change; it needs a baseline set at the start and a target. Good indicators are SMART: Specific, Measurable (quantity, quality, location), Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. They can be disaggregated by gender, age or location. Means of verification are the data sources; keep them simple and cost-effective. The logframe is a living document.

    High-level analyses and the charter

    This phase also makes early estimates. At minimum, the Guide asks for stakeholder, risk, human resource, supply chain and sustainability analyses, plus a first budget. Risks can be grouped into categories: strategic, financial, legal, organisational, political, environmental, technical and project management. The HR analysis looks at staff, partners, support staff and contractors, their roles, level of effort and capacity, and starts thinking about governance. The supply chain has three components: procurement, logistics and asset management. Sustainability is considered from the start, though humanitarian relief projects rarely have a sustainability element. The project should fit the program's Theory of Change, which is usually drawn at program level. Finally, the team drafts a project charter of three to five pages and, usually, writes the proposal.

    Project Set Up

    By now the funding is secured or under negotiation. Set Up prepares the team and stakeholders for planning, implementation and good governance, and it is the phase in which the project is officially authorized. Its question is simple: are we ready to start planning? If the project manager has not been hired yet, a focal point should run and document this phase.

    Set Up has four key outputs: the Project Charter, a comprehensive risk register, a stakeholder engagement strategy, and the project launch. It builds on the work from Identification and Definition, so its inputs are the initial risk and stakeholder assessments, the concept note or proposal, the high-level team capacity and resource assessments, and the logframe. At minimum it involves the governance (board, sponsor or steering committee), the project team and support staff, partners and contractors, the program manager and the beneficiaries.

    In practice, three things matter here. First, decision gates and stages: for longer projects, the manager draws a stage map, with a gate at the end of each stage. Second, internal controls: the processes that direct, monitor and measure resources, preventing and detecting fraud and protecting physical and intangible assets such as reputation. Third, steady engagement of stakeholders, including government, whose approvals go more smoothly when they have been involved all along.

    Risk

    The Guide defines risk as the potential effect of uncertainty on project activities, outputs and outcomes. Two ideas sit inside it: probability (how likely it is) and impact (how much it would hurt). A risk that actually happens becomes an issue. Most teams think only of negative risks, but there are also positive risks, opportunities worth "intelligent risk taking".

    Risk management has five steps: identify, categorize, assess, respond, and monitor and control. The first two began in Identification and Definition; Set Up concentrates on assessment and response. Assessment does two jobs: it prioritises risks by probability and impact, and it sets risk tolerances, deciding which risks are acceptable and which must be actively managed. The Risk Assessment Matrix plots probability against impact (low, medium, high) and draws a tolerance line: risks above it are actively managed, lower shaded ones are watched, and the rest are within tolerance. Write risks precisely, naming the effect on the triangle: "Rain interrupts latrine installation, causing a delay."

    The aim is not to remove every risk, which is impossible, but to respond when a risk exceeds tolerance. There are four responses. Avoid: don't do the risky part, or do it differently, such as running camp activities remotely through a local CBO instead of sending staff down a dangerous road. Transfer: shift or share the risk with another party, as with insurance. Mitigate: reduce the probability (more guards against theft) or the impact (keep only seven days of stock in the warehouse). Accept: live with the risk and keep monitoring it. Ignoring a risk is never acceptable.

    Risks are recorded in a risk register. At minimum it holds the risk, its status, probability and impact, the response strategy and the risk response owner, who is not always the project manager: in the Delta River register, a supply risk belongs to the logistics officer. The manager also sets a risk monitoring strategy that says when, how and with whom risks will be reviewed.

    Engaging stakeholders and governing the project

    The stakeholder engagement strategy goes beyond the earlier analysis. For each stakeholder it states their role in an activity, their interest, how they will be engaged, and what follow-up is needed. The Delta River example is a municipal official invited by letter and meeting to open the launch, then thanked and named in the press release.

    Governance is the framework in which project decisions are made. It clarifies authority, accountability, decisions on changes beyond the manager's tolerances, oversight of the project's viability, and support and advocacy. The simplest and most common structure is a single project sponsor, such as a program manager or INGO focal point; it is simple but misses many perspectives. Complex projects benefit from a project board or steering committee that represents donor, community, partner, government and organisational views. A board needs at least two members and commonly has three to five.

    The board decides on changes beyond the manager's tolerances, oversees the project, monitors its viability and can terminate it, represents its stakeholders, advises the manager, and advocates for resources. It is not a simple democracy: on a budget increase, for example, the donor's voice may decide. Smaller boards decide faster and better. The board meets regularly, reviews the risk and issue logs, and meets at every decision gate.

    Tolerances give the manager a clear zone of authority. The Guide names six: time, cost, scope, risk, quality and benefits (performance at outcome level). Suppose your tolerance is 5% of any budget line. An influx of displaced people means 20 more latrines and a 12% rise in that line: you must escalate it. A 2% rise in a component's price is yours to decide, but you still document and communicate it.

    Planning framework, MEAL framework, charter and launch

    Set Up also "plans the plan". The planning framework considers the project's length (stage planning suits projects of a year or more and conflict settings), the tools the team will use, and cross-cutting themes such as gender, protection and disability (for example, separate latrines within 50 metres for women, and wheelchair access).

    The MEAL framework sets up monitoring based on the logframe indicators, decides which evaluations to expect (real-time, final or ex-post), and plans accountability mechanisms and how feedback becomes learning. It also covers information management (storage, file naming, version control, security) and knowledge management (how knowledge is shared and used).

    The Project Charter pulls it all together: the project's purpose, deliverables, high-level estimates of activities, schedule, budget and roles, its risks, its tolerances, and its change control process. Signed by the governance, it authorises the start of work and the use of resources, creates a shared understanding, and records a shared commitment. It is a living document, re-signed after major changes. Without one, money is spent without support from decision makers, and stakeholders do not share an understanding of the project.

    The phase ends with the project launch, which formally marks the start, gives stakeholders a consistent understanding, and introduces them to the project. The charter is an ideal document to share, or a public version of it if parts are sensitive.

    Project Planning

    By the time planning starts, the team already has a logframe, a proposal and a charter. It is tempting to treat them as the plan. The Guide warns against this. A proposal is written to win approval and funding, in a donor's format, by a small team under time pressure, often months or years before work starts. An implementation plan is written to deliver the project on time, on scope, on budget and to quality, by the team and its stakeholders, in whatever detail they need. However long a proposal is, it is not a plan.

    The key output of this phase is therefore the implementation plan (sometimes called a DIP or PIP). It can be one document or several linked ones, and it typically has seven parts: a schedule plan, a risk plan, a MEAL plan, communication and stakeholder engagement plans, a RACI chart, a supply chain plan and a human resource plan. Its inputs are the logframe, proposal, charter, risk register, stakeholder analysis and engagement strategy, and the stage map.

    Planning should be participatory. The people who will do the work give better estimates, spot risks the manager would miss, learn the project as they plan it, and take ownership of it. Even opponents can be won over when their concerns shape the plan. Three other ideas run through the phase: accountability (transparency, protecting beneficiaries' data, participation, cross-cutting themes), control mechanisms (internal controls, change control so that only necessary and authorised changes are made, and quality control), and rolling-wave planning. Rolling-wave planning means adding detail to the plan as you go; at the start of each period the team confirms the logic and risks, updates activities and resources, and focuses on the most urgent threats. It is useful in emergencies, but it suits any project.

    Scope and the five steps of schedule planning

    The Guide separates two kinds of scope. Product scope is what the project will deliver or achieve: its outputs, outcome and goal, set out in the logframe during Identification and Definition. Project scope is how it will get there: all the direct and indirect work, set out in the WBS during Planning. Poorly defined scope causes unclear expectations, inaccurate estimates and scope creep, which is prevented by documenting scope and controlling changes formally. Planning for risk is everyone's job, not only the manager's.

    The schedule is built in five steps, each with its own tool.

    1. Activity definition uses the Work Breakdown Structure (WBS): a hierarchy that breaks the work into work packages. It must include indirect work such as hiring staff, baseline surveys, monitoring and IT set-up. It lists the work but does not put it in order. It can be drawn graphically or written in indented form.
    2. Activity sequencing uses a network diagram: boxes for activities and arrows for dependencies, showing which tasks must wait for others and which can run in parallel.
    3. Activity resource estimating decides who and what each task needs. It comes before duration estimating because resources drive time: one digger is slower than five. Budget, time pressure, labour rules, the weather and material availability all shape the choice.
    4. Activity duration estimating adds durations to the network diagram and reveals the critical path.
    5. Schedule development turns it all into a Gantt chart.

    Costs can be estimated three ways. Top-down estimates start from a global figure that a few experts split across work packages. Bottom-up estimates come from the people who know the field, and are rolled up; they take more effort but are more likely to be accurate. Parametric estimates use statistics from past projects, such as a cost per kilometre of road, and are only as good as the data behind them. An activity-based budget needs a complete list of activities and the cost of what each one requires.

    The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks through the network; it sets the shortest possible duration of the project, 29 days in the latrine example. Tasks on it have zero float. Float (or slack) is how long a task off the critical path can slip without delaying the end date; in the example, procuring the cap materials has three days of float. The Gantt chart, also called the work plan, shows each activity as a bar with its start, end and duration. A summary Gantt suits the board and donors; a detailed one suits the team.

    MEAL, controls, communication and roles

    Monitoring tracks the operational work at activity and output level, continuously, through field visits, records and reports. Evaluation looks at outcomes and the goal, periodically, often with external evaluators. There are three kinds of evaluation: real-time (during the project, so it can still improve), final (at the end, often required by the donor), and ex-post (some time after closure, perhaps a year, to see whether the impact lasted). Plan evaluations now, not at closure. Accountability means mechanisms for stakeholders to give feedback, such as posters or hotlines, and learning means planned moments for the team to reflect. A MEAL plan usually includes an indicator performance tracking table, a performance monitoring plan, feedback and accountability mechanisms, an evaluation plan and a learning plan. Every monitoring system needs indicators, a schedule and budget, responsible staff, a full data cycle (collect, review, summarise, analyse, feed back), data management, and a link to the program level.

    Internal controls promote efficient operations, reliable results, legal compliance, protection of resources, and lower risk of fraud. They cover HR, procurement, finance (with segregation of duties), inventory, contracts, infrastructure, security, vehicles and information. They give reasonable, not absolute assurance, and too many controls slow everything down.

    The communication plan says who needs what information, how, how often, and from whom, for example a monthly report to the donor. The RACI chart clarifies roles for each task: Responsible (does the work), Accountable (approves it, and there is only one), Consulted (two-way communication) and Informed (one-way updates).

    Supply chain, people, stages, sustainability and closure

    The supply chain plan is built with the supply chain team. Procurement planning defines the items, the process to buy them, and the delivery schedule. Logistics means the right thing at the right place at the right time; it covers inventory and warehousing and transport. Assets are managed by a policy that defines what counts as an asset; UNDP, for example, uses a value of USD 1,000 and a life of three years. The HR plan sets out who is needed and when, staff development, evaluation and motivation, since recruitment and turnover cause many delays.

    Long or complex projects use stage plans. The first stage is planned in detail and later stages are kept at a high level; each is detailed as it approaches, after a decision gate at the end of the previous stage. In humanitarian work, each distribution can be a stage.

    Sustainability planning and closure planning go together. The Transition Planning Matrix lists six components: plan for closure from the start, build partnerships and local links, build local capacity, mobilise local and external resources, stagger the phase-out, and let roles evolve after transition. The closure plan covers evaluations, reporting requirements, contractual, financial and administrative closure activities, and any handover.

    Project Implementation

    Implementation is the most intense phase. The manager juggles the triple constraint, risks and issues all at once, and almost everyone is involved: the team, governance, the program manager and most stakeholders. How smoothly it goes depends largely on how well the earlier phases were done.

    The phase produces an issue log, monitoring reports, possibly a real-time evaluation, narrative and financial reports, new stage plans and an updated risk plan. Its main input is the implementation plan and its subsidiary plans: risk, MEAL, HR, and stakeholder engagement and communication. Two practices keep the plan alive: rolling-wave planning, of which stage planning is one tool, and regular decision gates that review MEAL data, risks, finances and issues.

    Managing people

    "People make projects", so managing people may be the manager's most important and most difficult job. It needs the art (motivating, listening, resolving conflict) and the science (choosing tools, managing variances). The Guide highlights three tasks: developing the team (better skills, a reason to stay, stronger capacity), performance assessments, and keeping good team communication norms.

    Managing the schedule

    When the schedule slips, the usual fixes are to move deadlines or cut scope. When both are fixed, there are two techniques. Fast tracking runs in parallel activities that would normally happen one after another, starting with those on the critical path. Building the latrine caps while the holes are being dug cut the critical path from 29 to 26 days. Crashing adds resources to critical-path activities. Adding a second digger cut digging from three days to one and the path from 29 to 27 days. Doubling resources rarely doubles productivity, though: there may not be room in the hole for two people, or enough tools.

    Managing the budget

    The manager needs three financial skills: building budgets, estimating costs, and monitoring budgets and spending. Even without full control over finance, the manager remains accountable for it, working closely with the finance team on expenditure data, variances, payments, authorisations, cash flow and purchasing.

    The usual first question, "are we over budget?", is often answered badly. In the Guide's example, the project planned to spend 1,100 by month three but spent 1,300. That might mean activities cost more than expected, which is a problem. Or it might mean the project is ahead of schedule, which is not. To tell the difference, watch two things: cash flow and earned value. Earned Value Analysis compares planned and actual cost for each task and progress against the schedule. Here, 1,300 was spent to complete 1,300 worth of work: the project was exactly on budget and about 18% ahead of schedule. In the Guide's table, under budget and on or ahead of schedule is good; over budget and on or behind schedule is bad; under budget but behind, or over budget but ahead, needs more data. Earned value needs an accounting system that links activity costs to the schedule, which many development organisations lack.

    Managing risks and issues

    The risk register is reviewed at agreed points, and risk should be on the agenda of every project meeting, because risks change as the project matures.

    An issue is an unresolved decision, situation or problem that will significantly affect the project and cannot be solved immediately; in other words, a risk that has happened. Issue management is a team effort: the people closest to the work usually know best how to fix it, and each person resolves what is within their tolerance and escalates the rest. But the project manager is ultimately accountable. The process has four steps: identification and tracking, analysis, communication, and control. Left unresolved, issues cause missed deadlines, poor quality, a damaged reputation and later disputes.

    The main tool is the issue log, which records each issue, who reported it, who it is assigned to, its status and its resolution. In the Delta River log, rain stopped the digging for more days than the site supervisor's five-day tolerance allowed, so the issue went to the project manager, who added workers to crash the schedule.

    MEAL during implementation

    Monitoring is most important now, because it informs decisions and spots problems early. The Indicator Performance Tracking Table (IPTT) shows each indicator's baseline, its targets and actual results per period, and its endline value. The plan was to build 50 latrines in each of two quarters; the project built 35 and then 58, a total of 93, seven short. The rain issue explained the gap, and the shortfall in outputs also slowed the outcome, the increase in latrine use. Issues, MEAL and the schedule are tightly linked.

    A real-time evaluation can take a deeper look while there is still time to adjust. Accountability has four components: transparency (reporting and sharing information), standardization (following rules and MEAL standards), responsiveness (feedback mechanisms that are collected, sorted and acted on), and participation. Learning should be participatory, documented, shared and passed up to the program and portfolio.

    Managing change

    Change is normal, even desirable; a plan is a means to an end, not an end in itself. Teams that treat plans as fixed fail to see that the plan is flawed, fear telling the donor, and don't know how to update it. Yet changes must not be made casually. Every change must be managed through a formal process, analysed for its effects, documented across all parts of the plan, and communicated to stakeholders. Before approving a change, check that it is allowed under existing agreements, that its effect on schedule, cost and quality has been assessed, that stakeholders have been consulted, that the plan has been updated, and that resources are in place. A change request process map helps, but it must be adapted to the project's governance, donors and contracts.

    Managing the supply chain

    The project manager keeps ultimate responsibility for finance, supply chain and HR, even without line authority over those staff. Procurement runs from requisition to approved invoice and may buy materials, consultants, or whole services from suppliers. Its three steps are: update and review the procurement plan, identify providers, and select, negotiate and award. A Request for Estimates suits simple purchases decided mainly on price, but the lowest quote is not always the best value. A Request for Proposals or Invitation for Bid, with a clear statement of work, suits complex choices. Logistics covers inventory and warehousing (keeping minimum stock to cover lead times) and transport. Asset management has four elements: recording, labeling, monitoring records with physical counts, and safeguarding.

    Finally, the sustainability plan is revisited at decision gates, using evidence from MEAL and the issue log to check that the exit strategy is still valid.

    Project Closure

    A project is temporary by definition, with a beginning and an end. In development work, though, the end is often more of a transition: one phase hands on to the next, or a partner carries the work forward. Closure is often rushed and under-funded, as staff move on to new projects, so the Guide insists it is planned from the very first phase and built into the implementation plan. Its reflection is blunt: you can do everything else well, but a poor closure is what people remember.

    Five ways a project can end

    The Guide lists five closure scenarios. In a closure, the project formally ends and all closure activities are completed. In a handover, the product or service continues under a local partner: a local NGO, an INGO, the community or a government body. An extension adds time to finish, either with extra funding or at no cost. An expansion replicates parts of the project in a new area or with a new population. A redesign continues the work in a new phase with modified activities. Extensions are rarely planned; they usually follow changed circumstances or weak design.

    A no-cost extension deserves special attention. It adds time at no extra cost to the donor, but it still costs the organisation salaries, facilities and resources. Its impact on the triple constraint must be examined, and governance must be involved in the decision.

    Outputs, inputs and people

    Closure has four key outputs: the sustainability plan, the final reports, the final evaluation (when one is required), and lessons learned. Its inputs are the detailed implementation and stage plans, MEAL reports, narrative and financial reports, the issue log and the updated risk register; at minimum, the implementation plan and the monitoring reports. Most stakeholders are involved, from support teams and suppliers to beneficiaries and partners. Their attention is drifting to new work, so keep engaging them until the very end.

    All five principles show up here. Participation brings stakeholders into closure and lessons learned. Comprehensiveness makes sure every activity, including closure itself, is planned and completed. Good governance secures formal acceptance of the deliverables. Adaptation feeds the lessons into future projects.

    Sustainability and handover

    Sustainability has been planned since the beginning; now everything must be in place so that the partner, government or community can carry on. The Delta River example makes the point: the community will only keep using the latrines if the municipality has the equipment to maintain them. The Guide's sustainability checklist has six items: resources (people, equipment, technology), capacity (the knowledge and skills of whoever takes over), risk and response (walking the new owner through likely risks), stakeholders (introducing everyone who must be involved), processes and networks, and motivation. Motivation is the hardest, because it requires real buy-in, built through involvement during the whole project. Humanitarian relief projects rarely aim for sustainability, but they can still use resources efficiently and limit waste.

    Reporting

    "If it isn't reported, it didn't happen." Reporting is indirect work that must be planned and resourced. Closure usually requires three reports. The final narrative report summarises the project, its results against targets and indicators, its challenges, its lessons and what happened to its assets. The final financial report accounts for how the money was used, with supporting documents. The endline report, usually produced by the MEAL team, is compared with the baseline to show how far targets were met. Findings should also be shared with stakeholders, especially the community.

    Closing procedures

    Imagine the project being audited two years from now: would everything be in order? Closure has three parts. Contract closure confirms that all contracts are closed (suppliers, subcontractors, partners, donors) and that the donor has reviewed and accepted the deliverables. Financial closure confirms that all permitted funding has been received, advances have been settled, all bills paid, and assets disposed of correctly; poor asset disposal can lead a donor to refuse costs or demand repayment. Administrative closure releases or reassigns staff, reallocates or disposes of equipment and offices, and completes the reports and archives. Donors usually require records to be kept, on paper and digitally, for three, five or ten years, as the contract states.

    The scope itself is verified in two steps. First, the team checks the completed work against the implementation plan. Second, it meets key stakeholders such as the donor and community groups to review what was achieved, record their formal acceptance, and confirm they are satisfied with the outcomes as well as the technical work.

    Evaluation and lessons learned

    A final evaluation is not always done at project level, but when it is required it should have been planned and budgeted from the start. Its results are shared, and at program level the project's outcomes become the program's outputs. An ex-post evaluation needs documentation and data prepared before the team leaves.

    Lessons learned are the organisation's "memory bank". Collect them throughout the project, for example at decision gates and by adding a lessons learned column to the issue log, and make sure they are filed, shared and actually used, including with others in the sector. A handy tool is the After Action Review, a quick, cheap and participatory conversation that asks: What did we set out to do? What did we achieve? What went well? What could have gone better? What can we learn? It differs from an evaluation, which is formal, planned and resourced. Finally, a closing meeting or event recognises what was achieved and who made it possible.

    The five principles

    The phases tell you what to do and when. The principles tell you how to approach the work. Project DPro has five: Well-Governed, Participatory, Comprehensive, Integrated and Adaptive. Each runs through all five phases, and the exam asks both what each principle means and how it shows up in each phase.

    Well-Governed

    Governance is often vague, and project managers know they need it without knowing how to set it up. A well-governed project has clear lines of authority, so everyone knows who decides what and when, and it gives the manager a network of support. The governance structure ensures the organisation is committed and "owns" the project; it decides on changes beyond the manager's tolerances, oversees and resources the project, monitors whether it is still viable and can stop it, advises the manager, brings in different stakeholder perspectives, and advocates for support.

    Across the phases, it looks like this. In Identification and Definition, the team considers whether a sponsor, board or steering committee fits, and checks that the project aligns with program and portfolio strategy. In Set Up, that structure is formalised in the charter and the tolerances are approved. In Planning, the manager plans communication, decision gates and risk tolerances with governance. In Implementation, governance handles issues and risks above tolerance and controls change. In Closure, governance joins lessons learned with its "bird's-eye view" and signs off the updated charter. A simple example: if rain delays digging by five days and the manager's tolerance is three, the issue goes to the board.

    Participatory

    A good manager knows when and how to involve stakeholders. Participation builds trust, ownership and a shared vision, and lays the foundations for managing expectations, thorough planning, clear communication, sustainability and engagement. It does not mean consulting everyone about everything or deciding by consensus. It means bringing the right voices in at the right time and explaining how their input will be used.

    In Identification and Definition, participation shapes data collection, needs and problem analysis, and early estimates (bring in finance, HR and the program manager). In Set Up, it enriches risk identification, since beneficiaries and support teams see risks the manager would miss, and the launch. In Planning, the people who will do the work help build the WBS and estimates, and stakeholders help plan sustainability, which makes it more relevant and builds their capacity to take over. In Implementation, they take part in change control, decision gates and iterative planning. In Closure, they join lessons learned and the after action review, and a closing meeting or event recognises their contribution.

    Comprehensive

    Comprehensive management means giving equal rigour to every phase and to all the direct and indirect work, so that nothing is dropped. The Guide calls it a juggling act. In Identification and Definition, it means thorough stakeholder and needs analysis. In Set Up, a complete risk system with set points for reassessment. In Planning, a plan that includes indirect work such as recruitment, procurement and reporting, which so often cause delays. In Implementation, managing every component, not just the urgent one, and continuously monitoring risk alongside MEAL. In Closure, officially closing every activity, which also protects the organisation's reputation.

    Integrated

    Integration combines separate parts into a harmonious whole, so that phases, tools and people do not work in silos. In Identification and Definition, the key is team integration: bringing finance, security, HR, IT, MEAL and supply chain staff in early. In Set Up, it is risk integration: finishing the risk analysis so it can feed into planning. In Planning, support staff check budgets and supply plans; the manager balances the triple constraint; and the WBS stays consistent with the logframe while adding the detail the logframe lacks. In Implementation, the team works like pieces of one puzzle, including implementing partners. In Closure, the sustainability plan pulls all elements together, and lessons and evaluations become part of the organisation's memory.

    Adaptive

    No project exists in a vacuum, and no plan is perfect. Being adaptive means analysing the environment and responding with flexibility, guided by data. The tools that make a project adaptive include MEAL (monitoring shows when targets are slipping; accountability mechanisms bring feedback to learn from), integrated change control, and decision gates, where the stakeholder analysis, risk analysis, logframe, charter and implementation plan are revisited. Many Project DPro documents are "living" for exactly this reason. The cycle is Plan, Do, Learn, Review.

    In Identification and Definition, adaptiveness means gathering many perspectives, because needs differ and change, especially in humanitarian work. In Set Up, the MEAL framework, governance and risk analysis are the adaptive tools. In Planning, iterative planning in stages, with gates between them, is the adaptive approach. The Guide also describes Agile project management: time is fixed in short iterations, cost is fixed, and scope is variable. Requirements are ranked as must have, should have, could have and won't have this time, and only the must-haves are guaranteed. Agile needs close collaboration with users; its risks include losing the bigger picture and higher costs. In Implementation, decision gates, emergency decision gates, issue and change management, and lessons learned keep the project responsive. In Closure, the team asks whether changes were driven by data and whether lessons were used throughout.

    Adapting the Project DPro

    Tools achieve nothing if the team cannot make them work in its real environment. There is no single road map: every project is unique, and applying tools without thinking about context, resources and relationships produces a robotic, template-driven project that creates extra work and confuses staff and partners.

    The Guide tells of two managers who came back from Project DPro training. One was told, "Those tools are fine, but we don't do it that way here." The other was told, "Decide which tools you want and implement them on your own." Both situations should be avoided. Instead, the manager should assess the tools, choose the most useful, and work out with the organisation how they fit existing systems. Will a new tool complement or replace an old one? How will its information flow into current processes? Must those processes change? For each tool, ask: can I implement this now, or do I need more organisational support? A WBS or RACI can usually start at once; an agreed charter format may need the organisation's backing.

    Factors to consider

    Projects live inside programs, portfolios, organisational systems and donor structures, and all of these shape how the tools are used.

    Program considerations. A well-run program uses consistent tools and methods, sometimes through a Program Management Unit or Office, and projects should align with its guidelines. The Guide also notes that organisations often design large, complex projects when a program of smaller, simpler projects would work better.

    Systems considerations. Managers rarely choose their organisation's systems, but they must make information flow to meet the project's needs. Donors often want activity-based budgets while NGO accounts track line items, so the work of translating between them must be planned. On currency, the manager should insist that budget and expenditure use the same currency where possible, and otherwise apply the actual exchange rate rather than an easy book rate.

    Size, complexity and risk. A small, simple project does not need the same treatment as a multi-million, multi-site project in an insecure area. A simple qualitative risk register suits a low-risk project; a high-risk one needs a quantitative register and clear rules on who updates it and when. Small projects can share a governance structure, such as a program board, while a large one needs its own Project Board with a Senior User, a Senior Supplier and a Project Executive, clear terms of reference and members who understand their roles.

    Learning and competency. The manager must check that staff and partners can actually use the chosen tools, close gaps through training, and not expect to fix every weakness at once. PM4NGOs offers a competency assessment tool. A WBS introduced to a team that does not understand it is likely to fail.

    Performance. Training is not a one-off event; it should change on-the-job performance and contribute to real outcomes. One NGO asked every trainee to write a 12-month application plan, followed up every three months, and started with a small toolbox of four tools: the RACI, risk register, WBS and issues log.

    The Guide ends with a warning. Project management is as much art as science. Requiring every tool on every project, uniformly and without thought, could be a huge mistake. Good managers analyse each project and choose the right tools carefully and together with their teams.

    What changed in the 2nd edition

    Section 5 summarises the changes. Because PM4NGOs now has a separate Program DPro guide, Project DPro focuses more on the activity and output levels. The lifecycle changed in its number of phases, its presentation and its principles. Monitoring and evaluation now include accountability and learning and form a cross-cutting theme rather than a phase. Disciplines and their tools have been built into the phases, and the principles now align with Program DPro.
